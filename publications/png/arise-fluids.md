## CLINICAL QUESTION
In patients with early septic shock after initial fluid resuscitation, do earlier vasopressors and less subsequent IV fluid improve outcomes versus a more fluid-intensive approach?
## STUDY SNAPSHOT
- Multicentre, open-label randomized trial. Intention-to-treat population: 963.
- Comparison: earlier vasopressor / restricted additional fluid versus more liberal fluid / later vasopressor.
- Primary outcome: days alive and outside hospital at 90 days.
## ACTUAL RESULTS
- Additional IV fluid exposure at 24 hours was approximately 1.1 L lower with early vasopressors.
- Median days alive and outside hospital at 90 days: 76 days in both groups.
- Pulmonary oedema reported in 0.6% versus 5.0%; open-label ascertainment limits certainty.
- No demonstrated patient-centred outcome superiority of either overall strategy.
## CRITICAL APPRAISAL
- Strength: substantial multicentre randomization and patient-centred primary outcome.
- Limitation: open-label care and heterogeneous circulatory physiology; pulmonary oedema assessment may be influenced by knowledge of treatment.
- Neither strategy withheld all initial resuscitation fluid. Subgroup physiology was not individually tested as a treatment allocation tool.
## PHYSIOLOGY REVIEW
- MAP approximates cardiac output multiplied by systemic vascular resistance; low MAP alone does not diagnose inadequate preload.
- In vasoplegia, norepinephrine addresses vascular tone and may improve venous return.
- In preload-dependent low flow, carefully assessed fluid may be required. Responsiveness alone does not mean clinically needed.
## RESUSCITATION IMPLICATION
- Treat immediate life-threatening hypotension; use serial perfusion assessment, not MAP alone.
- Ask whether inadequate perfusion persists, then examine flow, venous congestion and likely vasoplegia before another bolus.
- Bottom line: use balanced, individualized fluid and vasopressor therapy rather than an automatic fluid-first or pressor-first rule.
